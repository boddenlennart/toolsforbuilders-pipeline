# What Bitcoin Does to Your Mind
*On taking it seriously as an idea, not a price*

*Published: Saturday, February 28, 2026*
*Status: APPROVED — add one personal memory before publishing*

---

Most people assume the important moment is when you decide to buy. It isn't.

The moment that matters comes later, often without warning. It's when you realise the question has changed. Not *what is this worth?* but *what does ownership actually mean?*

For me, it happened during a conversation I didn't expect to remember. Someone asked why I bothered with self-custody. "Just leave it on the exchange," they said. "It's safer there."

I couldn't immediately explain why that sentence felt wrong. Not inaccurate. Wrong. Like someone handed me a map with the compass directions switched. It took weeks before I could articulate the problem: we were using the same word — *yours* — to describe two fundamentally different relationships with reality. And once I understood that difference, I couldn't stop seeing it everywhere.

---

**The ownership question**

We live inside a consensus about ownership that most of us have never examined.

When you hold a stock, a bond, a balance in a savings account, what you actually hold is a claim. A promise recorded in someone else's ledger. The value might be real, but your access to it runs through institutions you've agreed to trust, usually by clicking a box you didn't read. This arrangement is so universal it's become invisible. It's the water we swim in.

Bitcoin breaks the surface.

For the first time, it's possible to hold something of value in a way that requires no intermediary, no permission, no ongoing relationship of trust. Access is secured by mathematics and physics. Not by a company's continued solvency. Not by a government's continued goodwill.

This distinction between holding a claim and possessing the thing itself sounds like a technicality. It isn't. It's the difference between "this is yours, as long as we say so" and "this is yours, and no one has standing to disagree."

Once you see that distinction, you can't unsee it. You start tracing the chains. Who actually controls the thing you think you own? Who can say no? Most people, when they follow the thread honestly, find a longer chain of intermediaries than they expected. And realise they've never once questioned any of them.

---

**The trust question**

Trust is not inherently bad. We extend it constantly, and most of the time it works.

But trust has a cost we rarely price in. When you depend on another party for access to your own resources, you've given them the power to revoke that access. This power may never be exercised. It may sit dormant for a lifetime. But it exists, and its existence is not neutral.

Banks have frozen accounts. Payment processors have refused transactions. Governments have seized assets under laws written broadly enough to apply to almost anyone. These aren't conspiracy theories. They're documented history, searchable, verifiable. The people affected weren't usually criminals. They were ordinary people who encountered the leverage that comes with dependence.

What Bitcoin clarified for me is not that institutions are malevolent. It's that dependence carries structural risk, and we've built our entire financial lives on dependencies we never consciously chose.

The protocol has no opinion about you. It doesn't know your name, your politics, your nationality. It validates work and enforces rules that no single actor can change. That's not a utopia. It's an engineering property. But the implication is significant: for the first time in history, it's possible to hold value without trusting anyone at all.

That idea refused to stay in the financial domain. At least, it didn't for me.

---

**The proof of work question**

Bitcoin's consensus mechanism is called Proof of Work.

To add a block to the chain, you must demonstrate that real computational work was performed. There is no workaround. No credential that substitutes for the effort. No way to simulate the output. The network either accepts the block or it doesn't.

I've found myself returning to this standard in contexts that have nothing to do with mining.

Am I actually strong, or do I just look strong? Did I build the discipline, or did I inherit a favorable situation and call it character? Is the progress I'm pointing to the product of genuine effort, or optimised for looking like effort to people who won't dig deeper?

These aren't comfortable questions. The network doesn't care about discomfort. It just requires the work. Applied to a life, that indifference is clarifying. The chain doesn't grade on a curve.

---

**The bleed**

What I didn't expect: these questions don't stay where you put them.

The ownership question bleeds into how you think about time. You have a balance. Hours per day, decades of working life. But who actually controls where those hours go? Who holds the keys to your attention, your energy, your capacity to say no?

The trust question bleeds into belief. Most of what people think they believe, they've never verified. They inherited it, absorbed it, accepted it on trust from sources they've never audited. If you've never stress-tested an opinion against real resistance, you don't own it. You're holding it on someone else's behalf.

The proof of work question bleeds into the body, the relationships, the choices you make when no one is watching. Is the result you're pointing to the product of genuine effort, or is it curated? Is there a gap between the signal and the substance?

Bitcoin didn't hand me answers to any of these. It gave me better questions. And a standard, rigorous and unforgeable, to hold those questions against.

---

**Why I'm writing this**

I'm not trying to convince anyone to buy anything.

I'm writing because the ideas underneath Bitcoin, ownership, trust, work, sovereignty, are worth thinking carefully about. Bitcoin happens to provide an unusually precise lens for that thinking, the way a well-designed tool reveals something about the nature of the problem it was built to solve.

If you came here looking for price predictions, you're in the wrong place. But if Bitcoin started making you ask different questions, about money, about time, about what it means to actually own something, then this is exactly where you should be.

Let's think it through.
